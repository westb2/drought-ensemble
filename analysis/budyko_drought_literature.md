# Literature: local Budyko / analogous framings for drought storage response

Saved from a literature search (Aug 2026) on whether temporary vs persistent storage deficits can be framed as a local energy- vs water-limited (Budyko-like) response, especially via overland / groundwater connectivity.

**Bottom line:** Pieces of this framing exist, but not the exact synthesis (temporary ≈ export-connected / energy-limited limb; persistent ≈ recharge-limited / water-limited limb, with overland participation as the spatial marker). Closest work falls in three clusters below.

Related project notes: [`budyko_temp_persistent_summary.md`](budyko_temp_persistent_summary.md), figures `analysis/figures/budyko_*.png`.

## Closest to a Budyko framing

### Within-catchment energy vs water limits via groundwater depth

**Very close conceptually**

- **Du, C., Sun, F., Yu, J., Liu, X., & Chen, Y. (2016).** Shift of annual water balance in the Budyko space for catchments with groundwater-dependent evapotranspiration. *Hydrology and Earth System Sciences*, 20, 3673–3690.  
  https://doi.org/10.5194/hess-20-3673-2016  
  Annual Budyko trajectories as a mix of **Budyko-type response in deep-WT zones** and **quasi–energy-limited behavior in shallow-WT zones** (ET can exceed P in dry years from GW supply). Nearest published statement of local energy- vs water-limited limbs inside one landscape.

### Budyko + storage / multiyear drought (usually catchment scale)

- **Mianabadi, A., Davary, K., Pourreza-Bilondi, M., & Coenders-Gerrits, A. M. J. (2020).** Budyko framework; towards non-steady state conditions. *Journal of Hydrology*, 588, 124926.  
  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhydrol.2020.124926  
  Review of extending Budyko when storage change is non-negligible.

- **Liu, Y., et al. (2023).** Water balance shifts induced by multiyear drought within the Budyko framework. *Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres*.  
  https://doi.org/10.1029/2022JD036758  
  Multiyear drought shifting P partitioning and catchment properties in Budyko space.

- **Condon, L. E., & Maxwell, R. M. (2017).** Systematic shifts in Budyko relationships caused by groundwater storage changes. *Hydrology and Earth System Sciences*, 21, 1117–1135.  
  https://doi.org/10.5194/hess-21-1117-2017  
  GW storage changes systematically shift Budyko points; spatial heterogeneity of GW–SW exchange matters.

- **Istanbulluoglu, E., Wang, T., Wright, O. M., & Lenters, J. D. (2012).** Interpretation of hydrologic trends from a water balance perspective: The role of groundwater storage in the Budyko hypothesis. *Water Resources Research*, 48, W00H01.  
  https://doi.org/10.1029/2010WR010100  

- **Wang, D., & related storage–ET Budyko extensions** (e.g. null-parameter storage–ET formulations). Example:  
  https://doi.org/10.1002/2017JD027758  
  Annual E/P depends on aridity and accessible storage S/P.

These support “drought memory lives in storage and moves points off the classical curve,” usually at **whole-catchment annual** scales — not cell-scale temporary/persistent maps.

### Lateral redistribution as a Budyko correction

- **Thompson, S. E., Harman, C. J., Konings, A. G., et al. (2017).** A Budyko framework for estimating how spatial heterogeneity and lateral moisture redistribution affect average evapotranspiration rates as seen from the atmosphere. *Hydrology and Earth System Sciences*, 21, 217–233.  
  https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/21/217/2017/  
  PDF: https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/21/217/2017/hess-21-217-2017.pdf  
  Lateral moisture redistribution changes average ET relative to ignoring connectivity. Local aridity alone is incomplete without routing; still about mean ET, not drought recovery deficits.

## Closest drought-response analogues (not Budyko-named)

### Fast vs slow recovery / storage spectrum

- **“The drought recovery spectrum: variable storage controls across a European climatic gradient”** (EGU 2026 abstract).  
  https://doi.org/10.5194/egusphere-egu26-14424  
  https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-14424.html  
  Modes: **connected** (immediate recovery), **threshold / soil-collector**, **buffered** (multi-month GW). Strong analogue to temporary vs persistent, framed as storage controls rather than Budyko.

- **Van Loon, A. F., et al. (2024).** Review article: Drought as a continuum – memory effects in interlinked hydrological, ecological, and social systems. *Natural Hazards and Earth System Sciences*, 24, 3173–3205.  
  https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/24/3173/2024/  
  Short memory in surface-dominated systems vs long memory / slow recovery in groundwater-dominated systems.

- **Peterson, T. J., Saft, M., Peel, M. C., & John, A. (2021).** Watersheds may not recover from drought. *Science*, 372(6543), 745–749.  
  https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abd5085  
  Some SE Australia catchments did not recover runoff fraction after the Millennium Drought despite rain returning (persistent low-runoff state; more P to ET).

- Related recovery / rainfall–runoff nonstationarity: Saft et al. and follow-ups on why runoff from some catchments does not recover (storage and GW–SW disconnection themes). Example discussion context:  
  https://doi.org/10.1002/hyp.70632  

- **Baseflow resilience / lagged GW drought** (review): Briggs et al.–style “James Buttle Review” on baseflow resilience across ecohydrological terrains.  
  https://doi.org/10.1002/hyp.70101  
  Lags between precip drought and baseflow; recovery can take years after quickflow returns.

### Landscape / water-table position (local, not Budyko)

- **Fan, Y., Miguez-Macho, G., Jobbágy, E. G., Jackson, R. B., & Otero-Casal, C. (2017).** Hydrologic regulation of plant rooting depth. *PNAS*, 114(40), 10572–10577.  
  https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1712381114  
  Topographic WTD gradient as water- vs drainage-limited continuum.

- **Costa, F. R. C., et al. (2023).** The other side of tropical forest drought: do shallow water table regions of Amazonia act as large-scale hydrological refugia from drought? *New Phytologist*.  
  https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.17914  
  Shallow-WT as drought refugia vs deep-WT exposure (ecological; same landscape limb).

- Classic **hydrologic landscapes** (Winter) and riparian vs upland drought-sensitivity literature sit in this family without Budyko language.

## What is *not* already out there (project niche)

> temporary storage deficit ≈ local energy-limited / export-connected limb

## Implication: should streamflow be drought-robust?

**Project framing corollary:** If streams (and high-overland cells) occupy the high water-availability / export-connected limb, then streamflow should look surprisingly robust to drought — even in catchments that do not look “drought-robust” by climate alone — because Q is generated from the wettest part of the landscape.

### What people have postulated (yes, in spirit)

**1. Storage attenuates meteorological drought into milder streamflow drought (short/moderate events).**  
Van Lanen / Van Loon / Peters drought-propagation work: stores cause **attenuation, lag, pooling, lengthening**. Quick systems see many sharp discharge droughts; slow GW systems filter minor events so streamflow can look buffered even when climate is dryish.

- Van Lanen, H. A. J., et al. Drought propagation through the hydrological cycle. IAHS.  
  https://iahs.info/uploads/dms/13647.26-122-127-67-308-Lanen-van.pdf  
- Van Lanen, H. A. J., et al. (2013). Hydrological drought across the world: impact of climate and physical catchment structure. *HESS*, 17, 1715–1732.  
  https://doi.org/10.5194/hess-17-1715-2013  
- Van Loon, A. F., et al. (2012). A process-based typology of hydrological drought. *HESS*, 16, 1915–1946.  
  https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/16/1915/2012/

**2. Streams are fed from connected / high-availability landscape nodes (baseflow resilience).**  
- Briggs, M. A., et al. James Buttle Review: The characteristics of baseflow resilience across diverse ecohydrological terrains. *Hydrological Processes*.  
  https://doi.org/10.1002/hyp.70101  
  USGS page: https://www.usgs.gov/publications/james-buttle-review-characteristics-baseflow-resilience-across-diverse-ecohydrological  
  Baseflow = drainage of dynamic GW storage that matters most in dry periods; springs / high-K corridor connections stabilize low flows. Also notes **non-intuitive feedbacks** (high connectivity can drain too fast in long dry spells).

- Discrete Riparian Inflow Points (DRIPs): stay **GW-connected even in extreme drought** while adjacent riparian zones disconnect.  
  https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frwa.2021.669007  

**3. Deep / long GW paths can insulate low flows from short climate swings.**  
- e.g. CONUS GW–stream connections (*Nature Water*):  
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s44221-024-00366-8  

So yes: literature argues **Q (especially baseflow) can be surprisingly resilient because it is sustained by the wet, connected end of the system**, not by domain-average aridity.

### Caveat (same literature — matches temporary vs persistent)

Resilience is **not unconditional**:

- Slow stores **lengthen and pool** drought: fewer small Q droughts, but **worse multi-year hydrological drought** once storage is depleted (Van Lanen 2013).
- High connectivity can **drain too fast** and fail to sustain low flows in long dry spells (Briggs review).
- After severe drought, runoff/baseflow may **not recover for years** even when rain returns (Peterson et al. 2021; Lee & Ajami–style baseflow drought lags).

**Link to this project:** near-stream **temporary** storage is the buffer that **pays for** Q during drought; it saturates quickly with drought length, while **persistent** upland deficits grow — so Q can look buffered early, then stay suppressed while much of the catchment is still “water-limited.”

I have **not** seen this written as: *streams occupy the energy-limited limb of a local Budyko, therefore Q is drought-robust.* Closest relatives are drought attenuation + baseflow resilience + DRIPs.

## Extension: rainfall–runoff ratio, peaks, and “both should be robust”

**Further corollary of the framing:** High-overland-flow areas *outside* the channel mask are also on the connected / high-availability limb. Those cells participate in runoff generation (saturation excess / quickflow). So not only baseflow, but **rainfall–runoff ratio and peaks** might also look relatively robust — or at least recover faster than deep storage — because storm runoff is generated from the wet, connected part of the landscape rather than from deep-WT uplands.

### Partial support in the literature

- **Quickflow vs baseflow recovery asymmetry:** Several papers note that **runoff / quickflow can return toward normal while baseflow remains depressed** for months–years after meteorological drought ends (Briggs review citing Lee & Ajami 2023; PPIC summary of baseflow drought). That is consistent with peaks/event runoff being tied to the fast, near-surface connected limb (your temporary branch), while low-flow resilience depends on slower stores (persistent branch).  
  https://www.ppic.org/blog/what-are-baseflow-droughts-and-why-should-we-care/

- **Drought attenuation of discharge deficits** in slowly responding catchments (Van Lanen et al.) is about filtering short events — a form of “Q robustness” for both hydrograph shape and deficit volume under moderate drought.

- **DRIPs / riparian connectivity** sustain lateral inputs across flow conditions, including drought — again, runoff generation focused in wet corridors.

### Apparent counter-literature (runoff ratio shifts / non-recovery)

These are the main papers that seem to contradict “runoff ratio is robust”:

- **Saft, M., Western, A. W., Zhang, L., Peel, M. C., & Potter, N. J. (2015).** The influence of multiyear drought on the annual rainfall–runoff relationship: An Australian perspective. *Water Resources Research*, 51, 2444–2463.  
  https://doi.org/10.1002/2014WR015348  

- **Peterson, T. J., Saft, M., Peel, M. C., & John, A. (2021).** Watersheds may not recover from drought. *Science*, 372(6543), 745–749.  
  https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abd5085  
  ~1/3 of SE Australia catchments still had lower runoff fraction 7 years after Millennium Drought; authors attribute leftover P to higher ET per unit P / persistent low-runoff state.

- Follow-ups on additional streamflow decline / mapping shifts (e.g. Saft MODSIM; Victoria non-recovery modelling). Farm dams and other human terms sometimes tested; Saft’s multi-model inference for *additional* decline during drought often favored catchment physical properties over farm-dam variables, but that does **not** fully clear human influence on post-drought states everywhere.

### Human confounders (your point — well taken, and discussed)

The non-recovery literature is **not** a clean test of natural connected-limb robustness. Confounders that can lower apparent rainfall–runoff ratio during/after drought:

| Confounder | How it biases Q/P downward |
|------------|----------------------------|
| **GW pumping** during drought | Intercepts water that would have become baseflow; deepens WTD; can disconnect streams; slows recovery (explicitly noted in baseflow-drought / California discussions). |
| **Farm dams / stock ponds** | Intercept hillslope runoff before the gage, especially in dry years when dams are empty and capture more of the next rain. |
| **Irrigation / return-flow changes** | Can either sustain or starve baseflow depending on source and timing (Briggs review). |
| **Land cover / “greening” / plantation** | Raises ET per unit P; often co-occurs with multiyear drought studies. |
| **Reservoir operations** | Alter low flows and peaks independently of hillslope generation. |

So: papers that report a persistent drop in rainfall–runoff ratio after drought **may partly reflect intensified human water use and interception during drought**, not only a natural shift of the connected runoff-generating limb. Your ParFlow drought ensemble (no pumping in the pure drought members) is a cleaner test of the natural connected-limb hypothesis than most observational SE Australia panels.

**Useful nuance for the framing (not a contradiction):**

1. **Peaks / event runoff** ↔ temporary / high-overland limb → should recover quickly once near-surface wetness and connectivity return.  
2. **Baseflow / annual Q/P in dry seasons** ↔ depends more on whether the slow store and GW–stream connection are intact → can look non-robust after long droughts even if storm peaks rebound.  
3. **Annual rainfall–runoff ratio** mixes both; a post-drought drop can be baseflow-dominated while peaks look fine — observers lumping annual Q/P may overstate “runoff generation failure.”  
4. **Human activity** can selectively hit baseflow (pumping) or peaks (farm dams), confounding both limbs differently.

### What would strengthen the claim

- In model (your setup): compare **peak / high-flow percentiles** vs **low-flow / baseflow** anomalies through drought and recovery; test whether high-overland cells’ contribution to Q recovers with the temporary limb while persistent upland deficits track low-flow suppression.  
- Against observations: restrict to minimally disturbed gages, or explicitly residualize pumping / farm-dam density before interpreting Q/P shifts (some Australian work tries; results mixed).  
- Separate **during-drought attenuation** (robustness while temporary storage is paying for Q) from **post-drought annual Q/P** (can stay low if GW connection or ET regime shifted).

Queries covered Budyko + drought/storage/energy–water limitation; hydrologic drought recovery + temporary/persistent storage + stream connectivity; local/spatial Budyko + landscape/WTD drought memory; streamflow/baseflow resilience + drought attenuation; rainfall–runoff non-recovery + farm dams/pumping confounders; quickflow vs baseflow recovery.
